# Lung Adenocarcinoma

## Definition
Lung adenocarcinoma is a specific type of Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC). It is the most common histological subtype of lung cancer, accounting for roughly 40% of all lung cancer cases. It arises from the glandular cells of the lungs, which normally secrete mucus and other substances. While it is strongly associated with smoking, it is also the most frequently diagnosed lung cancer in lifelong non-smokers. It typically presents as a peripheral mass in the lung.

## Histopathology Characteristics
Under a microscope, adenocarcinoma is characterized by its glandular differentiation or mucin production. Pathologists look for specific growth patterns:
*   **Lepidic Pattern:** Tumor cells proliferate along the surface of intact alveolar walls without stromal, vascular, or pleural invasion. This pattern is often associated with a better prognosis.
*   **Acinar, Papillary, Micropapillary, and Solid Patterns:** These indicate invasive growth and are associated with varying degrees of aggressiveness, with micropapillary and solid patterns generally having a worse prognosis.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC) plays a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis. Adenocarcinomas are typically positive for Thyroid Transcription Factor-1 (TTF-1) and Napsin A, and negative for squamous markers like p40 or p63.

## Common Molecular Mutations
One of the distinguishing features of lung adenocarcinoma is the high prevalence of actionable molecular driver mutations, especially in non-smokers. Identifying these is critical for targeted therapies.
*   **EGFR (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor):** Mutations in the EGFR gene are present in about 15-20% of adenocarcinomas in Western populations and up to 50% in Asian populations.
*   **ALK (Anaplastic Lymphoma Kinase):** ALK gene rearrangements or fusions are found in about 4-5% of cases, often in younger, non-smoking patients.
*   **ROS1:** Rearrangements occur in 1-2% of adenocarcinomas.
*   **KRAS:** KRAS mutations are found in 25-30% of cases and are more strongly associated with a history of smoking. Historically difficult to target, new therapies are emerging for specific KRAS mutations (like G12C).
*   **Other Targets:** Include BRAF V600E mutations, MET exon 14 skipping mutations, RET rearrangements, and NTRK fusions.

## Treatment Approaches
Treatment for lung adenocarcinoma depends on the stage, the presence of specific mutations, and the patient's overall health.
*   **Surgery:** The treatment of choice for early-stage (Stage I and II) and some Stage III adenocarcinomas. Procedures include lobectomy, wedge resection, or pneumonectomy.
*   **Targeted Therapy:** For advanced disease with identified driver mutations (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, etc.), targeted oral drugs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors or TKIs) are often the first line of treatment. They block the specific signals that cause the cancer cells to grow.
*   **Immunotherapy:** Immune checkpoint inhibitors (like pembrolizumab, nivolumab) block proteins (such as PD-1/PD-L1) that cancer cells use to hide from the immune system. It can be used alone or combined with chemotherapy, especially for patients without targetable mutations but with high PD-L1 expression.
*   **Chemotherapy:** Often used after surgery (adjuvant) to kill remaining cancer cells, or as a primary treatment for advanced stages, sometimes combined with immunotherapy or targeted therapy.
*   **Radiation Therapy:** Used for localized control, especially in patients who are not surgical candidates, or in combination with chemotherapy for Stage III disease.

## Prognosis by Stage
The prognosis for lung adenocarcinoma is heavily dependent on the stage at diagnosis.
*   **Localized (Stage I-II):** If the cancer is caught early and confined to the lung, the 5-year survival rate can range from 60% to over 80%.
*   **Regional (Stage III):** If it has spread to nearby lymph nodes, the 5-year survival rate drops to roughly 35-40%.
*   **Distant (Stage IV):** When metastasis to distant organs has occurred, the 5-year survival rate is historically low (around 7-10%). However, the advent of targeted therapies and immunotherapies has significantly improved survival and quality of life for many patients with advanced adenocarcinoma, with some patients living for many years.
