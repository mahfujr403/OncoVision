# Colon Adenocarcinoma

## Definition
Colon adenocarcinoma is the most common type of colorectal cancer, accounting for over 95% of cases. It originates in the glandular cells of the colon's inner lining (mucosa). These cells normally produce mucus to lubricate the intestinal tract. The cancer almost always begins as a benign growth called an adenomatous polyp, which, over years, accumulates genetic mutations and transforms into an invasive malignancy.

## Histopathology Characteristics
Diagnosis is confirmed via biopsy during a colonoscopy or from surgical specimens.
*   **Glandular Formation:** Under the microscope, the tumor cells form irregular, chaotic glandular structures that mimic normal colon crypts but invade deeply into the bowel wall.
*   **Grading:** It is graded based on how much the tumor resembles normal tissue. Well-differentiated tumors look much like normal glands; poorly differentiated tumors have lost their glandular structure and tend to grow and spread more aggressively.
*   **Mucinous and Signet Ring Features:** Some adenocarcinomas produce excessive amounts of mucin. Mucinous adenocarcinomas have abundant extracellular mucin, while signet ring cell carcinomas have mucin pushing the nucleus to the cell's edge. Both subtypes are often associated with a slightly poorer prognosis.

## Molecular Subtypes
Understanding the molecular genetics of colon adenocarcinoma is essential for determining prognosis and selecting treatments. There are two main pathways of genomic instability:
*   **Chromosomal Instability (CIN):** Found in about 85% of sporadic (non-inherited) colon cancers. It involves structural changes in chromosomes, leading to activation of oncogenes (like KRAS) and inactivation of tumor suppressor genes (like APC and TP53).
*   **Microsatellite Instability (MSI):** Found in about 15% of cases. It results from a defect in the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) system, causing mutations to accumulate in short repeating DNA sequences called microsatellites. MSI-High (MSI-H) or deficient MMR (dMMR) status is a hallmark of Lynch syndrome but also occurs sporadically. Patients with MSI-H tumors often have a better stage-for-stage prognosis and, critically, respond exceptionally well to immunotherapy.

Key specific mutations tested include:
*   **RAS (KRAS, NRAS):** Mutations occur in about half of cases. Tumors with RAS mutations do not respond to anti-EGFR targeted therapies (cetuximab, panitumumab).
*   **BRAF V600E:** Found in about 10% of cases, often associated with a worse prognosis but specific targeted therapies are available.

## Treatment
Treatment is multidisciplinary and based primarily on the clinical stage.
*   **Surgery:** The cornerstone of curative intent for Stage I-III disease. It involves removing the tumor segment of the colon and nearby lymph nodes (colectomy).
*   **Chemotherapy:**
    *   **Adjuvant:** Used after surgery for Stage III and high-risk Stage II disease to eliminate microscopic residual disease and reduce recurrence risk.
    *   **Common Regimens:** Standard regimens are typically based on 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and oxaliplatin. FOLFOX (5-FU, leucovorin, oxaliplatin) and CAPOX (capecitabine, oxaliplatin) are most common. For advanced disease, FOLFIRI (adding irinotecan) is also used.
*   **Targeted Therapy:** Used mainly for advanced (Stage IV) metastatic disease, usually combined with chemotherapy.
    *   **Anti-VEGF:** Bevacizumab blocks blood vessel growth to the tumor.
    *   **Anti-EGFR:** Cetuximab and panitumumab block growth signals but are ONLY effective if the tumor is RAS wild-type (no KRAS/NRAS mutation).
*   **Immunotherapy:** Immune checkpoint inhibitors (like pembrolizumab or nivolumab) are highly effective and now the first-line standard of care for advanced colon adenocarcinomas that are MSI-High/dMMR.

## Prognosis
Prognosis is heavily dependent on the stage at diagnosis.
*   **Localized (Stage I-II):** High cure rates with surgery alone, with 5-year survival rates around 90%.
*   **Regional (Stage III):** With surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, 5-year survival is approximately 70-75%.
*   **Metastatic (Stage IV):** The 5-year survival rate drops to about 15%. However, advances in surgical techniques (such as resecting isolated liver or lung metastases), better chemotherapy combinations, and the introduction of targeted and immunotherapies have steadily improved the median overall survival for metastatic patients from mere months to over 2-3 years, with some achieving long-term remission.
