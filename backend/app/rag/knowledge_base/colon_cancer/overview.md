# Colon/Colorectal Cancer Overview

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a cancer that starts in the colon or the rectum. These cancers can also be called colon cancer or rectal cancer, depending on where they start. Colon cancer and rectal cancer are often grouped together because they have many features in common. Most colorectal cancers begin as a growth on the inner lining of the colon or rectum, called a polyp. Over time, some types of polyps can change into cancer.

## Types of Colorectal Cancer
*   **Adenocarcinoma:** This is by far the most common type, accounting for about 95% of colorectal cancers. These cancers start in cells that form glands that make mucus to lubricate the inside of the colon and rectum.
*   **Carcinoid Tumors:** These start from specialized hormone-making cells in the intestine.
*   **Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors (GISTs):** These start from special cells in the wall of the GI tract called the interstitial cells of Cajal.
*   **Lymphomas:** These are cancers of immune system cells that typically start in lymph nodes but can also start in the colon, rectum, or other organs.
*   **Sarcomas:** These tumors can start in blood vessels, muscle layers, or other connective tissues in the wall of the colon and rectum.

## Epidemiology
Colorectal cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer in both men and women in the United States, excluding skin cancers. It is also the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths. The incidence rate has been dropping in older adults (largely due to screening), but it has been increasing in younger adults (under age 50).

## Risk Factors
*   **Age:** The risk increases as a person gets older. While it can happen at any age, most cases occur in people older than 50.
*   **Diet:** Diets high in red meats (such as beef, pork, lamb) and processed meats (such as hot dogs and some luncheon meats) can raise colorectal cancer risk.
*   **Physical Inactivity and Obesity:** People who are not physically active and those who are obese have a higher risk of developing colorectal cancer.
*   **Smoking and Heavy Alcohol Use:** Both are linked to an increased risk.
*   **Personal or Family History:** A history of colorectal polyps or colorectal cancer increases risk. Having a first-degree relative (parent, sibling, or child) with CRC increases the risk.
*   **Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD):** Conditions like ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease increase the risk.
*   **Inherited Syndromes:** About 5% of CRCs are caused by inherited gene mutations. The most common are Lynch syndrome (hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer, or HNPCC) and Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP).

## Screening
Regular screening is one of the most powerful weapons for preventing colorectal cancer. Screening can find precancerous polyps so they can be removed before they turn into cancer. It can also find CRC early, when treatment works best.
*   **Colonoscopy:** The gold standard. A doctor uses a long, flexible tube with a camera to view the entire colon and rectum. Polyps can be removed during the procedure.
*   **Stool DNA/Fecal Immunochemical Test (FIT):** Tests like Cologuard look for hidden blood and altered DNA in stool.
*   **Fecal Occult Blood Test (FOBT) / FIT:** Yearly tests that check for hidden blood in the stool.
*   **CT Colonography (Virtual Colonoscopy):** Uses CT scans to produce images of the colon.

## Symptoms
Colorectal cancer might not cause symptoms right away. When they do occur, symptoms can include:
*   A change in bowel habits, such as diarrhea, constipation, or narrowing of the stool, that lasts for more than a few days.
*   A feeling that you need to have a bowel movement that is not relieved by having one.
*   Rectal bleeding with bright red blood.
*   Blood in the stool, which might make it look dark brown or black.
*   Cramping or abdominal (belly) pain.
*   Weakness and fatigue.
*   Unintended weight loss.

## Staging
Staging is used to find out how far the cancer has spread.
*   **Stage 0:** Cancer is in its earliest stage and has not grown beyond the inner layer of the colon or rectum.
*   **Stage I:** The cancer has grown through the mucosa into the muscle layer of the colon or rectum.
*   **Stage II:** The cancer has grown through the wall of the colon or rectum but has not spread to nearby lymph nodes.
*   **Stage III:** The cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes but has not spread to other parts of the body.
*   **Stage IV:** The cancer has spread (metastasized) to distant organs, such as the liver or lungs.

## Survival Rates
Survival rates for colorectal cancer rely heavily on the stage at diagnosis. The 5-year relative survival rate for localized stage CRC is about 91%. If the cancer has spread regionally to surrounding tissues or organs and/or the regional lymph nodes, the 5-year survival rate is 73%. If the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body, the 5-year survival rate is around 13%. Early detection through screening is paramount for favorable outcomes.
